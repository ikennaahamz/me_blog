Saving something feels a lot like understanding it. I highlight a paragraph in a book, bookmark a thread, or finish a feature in code, and in the moment the idea feels completely mine. A week later, the highlight reads like a fragment from someone else's conversation, and the code tells me what I built without telling me what I was trying to avoid.

Storage is cheap now, which makes it easy to confuse an archive with a memory. I can collect quotes, links, and snippets faster than I can actually think through any of them. Most of those notes only prove I passed by an idea. They rarely show that I stayed with it long enough to test whether it changed how I think.

## Wait until the borrowed phrasing fades

When I take a note right after reading or listening to a podcast, I am usually still leaning on the author's momentum. Their phrasing sounds convincing, so I copy it and mistake recognition for clarity.

If I wait a few days and rewrite the note without looking back at the source, the weak spots show up immediately. Whatever I cannot explain in plain language, I did not really hold onto. A note worth keeping has to stand on its own: what question it answers, why it mattered to me, and where I might still disagree with it.

## Keep the reasoning, not just the result

The same habit shows up in software. A repository keeps the final code and quietly drops the trade-offs behind it. Months later, a simple choice—like keeping this blog static instead of building a database-backed app—can look arbitrary unless I recorded what constraints actually mattered at the time.

Writing that reasoning down gives my future self something honest to argue with. When requirements change, I can respond to the old thinking instead of guessing why I made the trade-off in the first place.

A good note slows me down just enough to find out what actually stayed—and leaves a trail I can still question a month later.
