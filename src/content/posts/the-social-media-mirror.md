Social media presents itself as a place of limitless possibility: a world where we can speak, learn, connect, and construct an identity of our own choosing. I have built a whole life in this online space—books saved for later, ideas gathered from strangers, and enough content to keep me occupied almost forever. At its best, a status can reveal an inner corner without exposing an entire private life: a place to share what interests us, invite criticism, and resist the pressure of current trends. Yet beneath this abundance lies an uncomfortable question: how much of this world actually belongs to me?

Everything online is competing for attention. Corporations, creators, advertisers, and ordinary people all want a claim on our limited awareness. The feed does not merely show us what we desire; it participates in creating that desire. It operates like a lottery of information: each refresh promises an uncertain reward, and that inconsistency keeps us returning. Even seduction becomes part of the machinery. Sex may sell, but its success also depends on the willing customer—on the person whose vulnerabilities have been studied, anticipated, and made profitable. The internet learns what lowers our defences and returns it to us as though it were simply what we had freely chosen.

This makes the promise of online autonomy difficult to trust. We are told that we can be anyone, follow anything, and express ourselves however we wish, but those choices take place inside systems designed by someone else. We increasingly view technology—and therefore the world—through a medium handed down by large corporations. Their algorithms shape our habits and ideology, often with our willing participation. The platforms give us their toys, allow us to become attached to them, and then remind us that we never truly owned them. Features disappear, archives become inaccessible, devices become obsolete, and nostalgia itself is placed behind another purchase. The command is always the same: keep consuming, keep upgrading, do not stop.

The deeper cost, however, is not economic but psychological. Social media turns the natural human need for recognition into a permanent public performance. When we are starved of attention, we search for acknowledgement outside ourselves and begin constructing an identity from other people's reactions. We polish the self in the hope that perfection will protect us from judgment: if I appear intelligent, attractive, moral, or interesting enough, perhaps I will be loved for the person I claim to be. Even knowledge becomes performative. We may learn to understand and build upon the world, or we may learn because being seen as knowledgeable carries social value. The distinction is clearest when no one is watching.

Performance itself is not new. Human beings have always adapted to groups, displayed belonging, and sought approval. Social media intensifies this old impulse by making the audience constant and its judgment measurable. Likes, views, and replies convert recognition into numbers, while algorithms surround us with the attitudes and performances that signal membership in a community. Representation becomes so polished that we can no longer reliably distinguish authenticity from advertising. We absorb these images as reality, reflect their standards back into the world, and are disappointed when life cannot meet them. Over time, we may mistake conformity for growth and visibility for character.

The same machinery affects our common life. A personalised feed can become a giant feedback loop in which we hear our own thoughts returned to us, strengthened by repetition. Politics is flattened into social performance: complex disagreements become content, identity signals, and opportunities for engagement. Division is packaged for daily consumption because outrage holds attention and attention produces economic value. Behind the frictionless interface, meanwhile, sits human labour that the mythology of Silicon Valley often erases. The celebrated lone genius obscures the workers, extraction, and unequal power upon which the digital world is built.

The answer is not to imagine that we can become completely independent of other people. We are hard-wired to seek acceptance, and community is part of being human. The task is to recover some distance between the self and the audience—to act outside the spotlight, to learn without needing to look wise, and to ask whether our desires remain ours when no one is there to validate them. A critical view of social media is not empty cynicism. It is a form of self-preservation: an attempt to remain awake inside systems that profit whenever we forget to ask who is shaping whom.
